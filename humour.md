# Humour Guide — House Voice for Blog Posts

> **When to use:** Apply this to every blog post (and client-facing web copy). Substance first, humour second — humour seasons the writing, it never load-bears.

---

## The Voice in One Line

Warm, self-aware, slightly goofy — happily makes itself the punchline, drops the occasional dad joke, winks at the reader, but **always lands back on the point.**

If a sentence wouldn't sound right coming out of Paul's mouth out loud, cut it.

---

## The Decoded Rules (from "Writing Humor: The Art of Making Readers Laugh")

The source article (preserved at the bottom) boils down to **5 working rules** plus a few craft notes. This is the operational version.

### 1. Be Yourself (authenticity over cleverness)
Readers can smell forced jokes. Use humour that's genuinely *ours* — dad jokes, a fondness for a slightly-uncool reference, a self-deprecating aside. Authenticity is what builds the audience, not the joke quality. **Litmus:** "Would Paul actually say this?" If no → delete.

### 2. Keep It Light-Hearted (no charged topics)
Stay away from anything controversial — politics, religion, anything that could offend. It's the client's reputation on the line. The more charged the comment, the more likely it alienates a slice of readers. Default to gentle, warm, low-stakes.

### 3. Universal Before Niche
Avoid hyper-specific in-jokes early. Keep references broad enough that almost anyone gets them. Once a reader knows our voice, *then* you can slip in a sharper, more specific zinger. New reader = universal; familiar reader = you've earned the niche.

### 4. Audience-Appropriate (PG-13 ceiling)
Hard cap at PG-13. No profanity, nothing crude. Beyond rating, match the joke to *who's reading* — what one group finds funny sails over another's head. Think about the actual reader of *this* post and calibrate. (See client calibration below.)

### 5. Sprinkle, Don't Slather
Humour is **not** Frank's Red Hot — you can't put it on everything. **Target ~1 light touch per section**, fewer in serious posts. Used sparingly, a joke becomes a memorable standout moment; used constantly, it exhausts the reader and buries the message. When in doubt, cut a joke.

### 6. Don't Be Afraid to Fail
Even great comedians bomb. Try the line; if it doesn't land in review, pull it. Small doses beat no doses.

## Signature Moves (reach for these — not all at once)

- **Self-deprecation** — make ourselves the punchline before anyone else can.
- **Dad jokes / light puns** — embraced, unironically.
- **Broad pop-culture asides** — universal, never obscure.
- **Bracketed stage directions** — e.g. *"(Narrator: it did not.)"* or *"[switches to serious face]"*.
- **Craft analogies** — vivid or mildly absurd comparisons that *teach while they amuse* (the article's "humour writing = throwing darts" is the model).
- **Gentle reader teasing** — a wink at the reader ("the fact that you've read this far…").
- **Casual, lightly-slangy register** — conversational, never stiff.

---

## Placement Map

**Good places for a joke:**
- The intro hook (earn the read)
- Section transitions
- Dry/technical passages (wrap the concept in an analogy)
- The sign-off (a callback to an earlier joke lands best)

**Keep humour OUT of:**
- Step-by-step instructions (clarity > comedy)
- Anything safety / legal / money / health-related
- Stats, claims, or anything a reader needs to trust at face value

---

## Pre-Publish Litmus

1. Would the author say it out loud, unembarrassed?
2. Could it offend or exclude any reader? (If maybe → cut.)
3. Does the paragraph still read fine if the joke were removed? (It should — humour is seasoning.)
4. More than 1–2 jokes in any one section? → trim.

---

## Client Calibration

- **Dial DOWN:** finance, legal, medical, corporate, B2B-serious.
- **Dial UP:** lifestyle, food, creative, local-service, personality-driven brands.
- Always confirm the client is comfortable with a casual register before shipping jokes in *their* copy.

## Source Article (reference — do not edit)

*"Writing Humor: The Art of Making Readers Laugh" — Mark Bowden, Aug 18, 2023*

> "I've got 99 problems, I feel bad for ya son…I've 99 problems but my humor ain't one!" As I write those words, all that I can think about is how embarrassed my 14-year-old son would be reading that opening statement, and it makes my feel goods go wild.
>
> Writing was not my initial career choice, I found it later in life, but it didn't take long to figure out that I love writing with humor, even if it is a bit "sus" at times.
>
> That's right, you jumped onboard the dad joke train, and I am here to be your spirit guide on your journey to tickling your reader's funny bone.
>
> [Switches to serious face] Now that you know what you are in for within this particular article. We need to have a serious discussion about why you would want to include humor in your writing and the mechanisms to help you connect with your audience.
>
> **Humor's Role In Writing**
> From a chuckle to a deep belly laugh like a baby that just saw a cat do a backflip, laughter is an amazing thing. Making your readers laugh is truly an art form that some get and others don't even try. I am here to tell you that inserting humor into your copywriting is a great way to ingratiate yourself with your audience and earn some street cred. You don't have to be Jack Black, Robin Williams, or Richard Pryor to get a laugh from your audience, you just have to find ways to connect with them that go beyond mundane text.
>
> **5 Tips For Writing With Humor**
> I like to equate writing with humor to throwing darts. When you first start out, you are happy to find the board. As you practice and perfect your craft, you get more and more accurate and are able to create copy that resonates. As you get started, you will miss the board from time to time, and that is OK. Even the best comedians have jokes that bomb.
>
> **Be Yourself** — When it comes to humor, the best thing that you can do is be yourself. If you aren't authentic in your approach or the humor that you use, your readers will be able to tell. The more of yourself that you can incorporate into your writing, regardless of style, the easier you will find it to build an audience.
>
> **Keep It Light-hearted** — Not all clients will appreciate a more casual tone or approach. They don't want to accidentally offend anyone as it is their business and reputation on the line. Keep it lighthearted and stay away from controversial topics. The more charged the comment, the more likely it will miss and alienate some segment of your audience.
>
> **Use Universal References** — If you are worried about missing the mark, avoid hyper-specific jokes. The more general you can keep it, the better. Over time, as your audience grows and becomes accustomed to your writing style, you can slowly work in some zingers that are more focused.
>
> **Use Audience Appropriate Jokes** — Any jokes within your copy should be rated no higher than PG-13. But audience-appropriate jokes have additional layers — such as life experiences. What one group finds funny may completely go over the head of another group. Think about who will be reading your article, and plan accordingly.
>
> **Sprinkle It On** — Humor isn't like Frank's Red Hot — you can't put that stuff on everything. Sprinkle it in here and there throughout the copy rather than slathering it on. When it is used sparingly, humor creates memorable moments within the text.
>
> **Master The Art Of Writing With Humor** — Don't be afraid to fail. You can't predict everything, and unless you try something new, you will never know if it resonates with your audience. Even if you write with humor in small doses, just for yourself, it can instantly improve your storytelling and copywriting skills.
